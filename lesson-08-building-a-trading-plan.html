Building a trading plan

Seven lessons only pay off if they become written rules you follow. This is where it all becomes one page.

8 min readLesson 08 of 8
Advertisement

Why a written plan beats a mental one

A plan turns a decision under pressure into “check the plan” instead of “figure it out right now”. A plan that only lives in your head bends the moment emotion arrives, which is exactly when it needs to hold.

1. Define your setups

Vague (“looks like it might go up”) is not a rule. Specific is: “price closes above the 50 EMA on H4, the daily trend is also up, and I enter on a pullback to that EMA.”

  • Which timeframes you trade
  • What trend and structure must be present (Lesson 3)
  • Which indicators, if any, confirm (Lesson 6)
  • What invalidates the setup before you enter

2. Write your risk rules in numbers

Risk per trade: ____ % of account (commonly 1–2%)
Max total open risk across all trades: ____ %
Max daily / weekly loss before I stop: ____ %
Minimum risk-reward I will take: 1 : ____

Fill the blanks before your next trade, not during it, and use the risk calculator to convert your percentage into a lot size every time.

3. Build a routine

WhenWhat you do
Before the sessionCheck the economic calendar for high-impact releases; note daily trend and key levels
Before each entryConfirm the setup matches your written rules; run the calculator; set the stop first
After each tradeLog it in the journal regardless of outcome
WeeklyReview the journal: which setups worked, which rules were broken and why

4. Keep a journal

Grab the journal template. The most useful line is the last one: did I follow my own plan? A losing trade taken by the plan is a normal cost of business. A winning trade taken by breaking the plan is a warning sign wearing a disguise.

5. Revise the plan, but not mid-trade

Plans should improve as your journal gives you data. Changes happen in the weekly review, calm and deliberate, never in the middle of an open position.
