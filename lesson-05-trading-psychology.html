Trading psychology

Two traders can run the identical strategy. One is profitable, one is not. The difference is almost never the strategy.

7 min readLesson 05 of 8
Advertisement

Why this is a separate lesson

Lessons 1 to 4 are mechanical. Following them trade after trade, especially after a loss or a big win, is a different skill. Most blown accounts are not a knowledge problem. They are a knew-the-rule-and-broke-it problem.

The common traps

FOMO

A pair moves without you and the pull to jump in late, off-plan, is strong. Chasing a move that has already happened is a reliable way to buy tops and sell bottoms.

Revenge trading

After a loss, re-entering immediately and bigger to win it back replaces analysis with emotion. The market does not know about your last trade.

Overconfidence after a streak

A few wins can feel like proof of skill, leading to oversized positions right before the streak ends. Dozens of trades tell you far more than a streak.

Moving the stop-loss

Giving a losing trade a little more room quietly breaks the position-size math from Lesson 4. The position was sized for that stop, not a wider one.

The pattern underneath all four

Each trap replaces a pre-decided rule with an in-the-moment feeling. The fix is not to feel less. It is to decide the rule in advance, when you are calm, and treat the moment as execution only.

Keep a trading journal

A simple log turns trading from a blur of outcomes into a dataset you can learn from. Use our journal template and record at minimum the setup, entry, stop, target, size, percent risked, and whether you followed your own plan.

Rules beat mood

Traders who last convert as much of the process as possible into rules decided in advance: what setups qualify, what risk per trade, what happens after a loss, what happens after three losses in a row.

Realistic expectations

Consistent profitability, if it comes, comes slowly, and drawdowns are normal even for good strategies. Anyone promising fast, guaranteed or effortless returns is describing something that does not match how markets behave. Treat that as a warning sign.
